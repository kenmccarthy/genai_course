Topic 1

Welcome

Why this matters — and why SETU is investing in it now.

⏱️ ~5 min📖 Read

Why AI matters

Generative AI has moved from a research curiosity to an everyday tool in the space of a couple of years. Students are already using it. Employers expect graduates to understand it. And it is quietly reshaping how teaching, research, and professional services get done across every university.

You don't need to become a technologist. You do need enough literacy to make good judgements: when a tool helps, when it doesn't, and where the limits and risks sit.

Why SETU is investing in AI

South East Technological University is investing in AI capability so that staff and students can benefit from these tools confidently and responsibly — with clear guidance, shared principles, and appropriate governance, rather than in an ad-hoc way. This module is the first step: a common baseline for everyone.

🎯 Key idea

The goal isn't to use AI for its own sake. It's to use good judgement about when and how it genuinely helps your work.

AI and the future university

The university of the next decade will treat AI fluency much as it treats digital literacy today: an expected, ordinary capability. Assessment, student support, administration, and research are all adapting. Being part of that conversation early — and shaping it — is better than reacting to it later.

AI is a colleague — not a replacement

Throughout this course, think of AI as a capable but unreliable assistant: fast, tireless, and surprisingly broad — but one whose work you must always check. It can draft, summarise, brainstorm, and explain. It cannot be accountable. You remain responsible for anything you produce with its help.

Topic 2

What is AI?

A simple map of the words people use — often interchangeably, and often wrongly.

⏱️ ~7 min🧩 Includes an activity

“AI” is an umbrella term. Underneath it sit several ideas that are related but not the same. Here's the plain-language version.

Artificial Intelligence (AI)The broad goal of getting computers to do things that would normally need human intelligence — recognising speech, spotting patterns, making decisions. The widest umbrella.
Machine Learning (ML)A way of building AI by letting a system learn patterns from examples instead of being programmed with explicit rules. Most modern AI is machine learning.
Large Language Models (LLMs)A type of machine-learning model trained on enormous amounts of text to predict likely words. This is the engine behind tools like ChatGPT, Claude and Copilot.
Generative AIAI that creates new content — text, images, audio, video, code — rather than just classifying or predicting a number. LLMs are the text-and-language kind of generative AI.
AgentsGenerative AI given the ability to take actions in steps — using tools, searching, or completing a multi-step task on your behalf, not just answering a single question.

🎯 One sentence to remember

Generative AI is machine learning that produces new content — and a chatbot is a large language model wrapped in a friendly interface.

Activity Which of these are AI?

Tap the ones you think count as AI, then reveal the answers. Some are trickier than they look.

The trick: AI isn't “anything a computer does”. It's specifically systems that learn from data or generate content, rather than following fixed rules a person wrote out.

Topic 3

How AI works

No maths. Just the handful of ideas that explain most of what you'll see.

⏱️ ~8 min▶️ Includes an animation

It's prediction, not knowledge

An LLM does one deceptively simple thing: given the text so far, it predicts the next chunk of text. Then it does it again, and again. It has no database of facts it “looks up” — it has learned, from vast amounts of writing, which words tend to follow which.

Watch it build a sentence one prediction at a time:

Probability

At each step there are many possible next words, each with a different likelihood. The model usually picks a likely one — but not always the single most likely, which is why the same question can give slightly different answers each time.

Tokens

The model doesn't read whole words — it reads tokens, small pieces of text (roughly ¾ of a word on average). “Understanding” might be two or three tokens. This is why AI tools have length limits measured in tokens, and why they sometimes miscount letters.

Context window

The context window is how much text the model can “hold in mind” at once — your prompt plus its reply plus the earlier conversation. Go beyond it and the earliest parts scroll out of view and are effectively forgotten. Long documents can exceed it.

Hallucinations

Because the model generates plausible text rather than retrieving verified facts, it can produce confident, fluent statements that are simply wrong — a made-up statistic, a fake citation, an invented quote. This is called a hallucination, and it's a feature of how the technology works, not a rare bug.

Reasoning

Newer models can “think” through a problem in steps before answering, which improves harder tasks like maths or logic. It's a real improvement — but it's still prediction underneath, and it can still be confidently wrong.

🎯 Key idea

An LLM is a very sophisticated autocomplete. That single fact explains its fluency, its creativity, and its most important weakness — that it can be wrong while sounding completely certain.

Topic 4

Capabilities

What today's tools are genuinely good at.

⏱️ ~5 min📖 Read

Modern general-purpose AI tools are strikingly versatile. Here's the range you're likely to meet:

✍️

Text

Drafting, summarising, rewriting, tone changes, structuring notes and emails.

🖼️

Images

Generating and editing images and diagrams from a text description.

🎬

Video

Producing short video clips and animations, and captioning or summarising footage.

💻

Coding

Writing, explaining, and debugging code — useful well beyond software teams.

🔎

Research

Explaining topics, surfacing angles, and (with web tools) gathering current sources.

📊

Analysis

Spotting themes in text, drafting first-pass analysis of data and documents.

🌍

Translation

Fast, fluent translation and localisation across many languages.

🎙️

Voice

Transcribing speech, reading text aloud, and holding spoken conversations.

ℹ️ A useful rule of thumb

AI is strongest as a first-draft engine and a thinking partner — getting you from a blank page to something you can react to. It's weakest as a final authority. The value is in the acceleration, not in blind trust.

Topic 5 · Most important

Limitations & risks

If you remember one topic from this course, make it this one.

⏱️ ~9 min🧩 Includes a self-check
⚖️

Bias

Models learn from human writing, so they absorb and can amplify its biases — around gender, race, culture and more.

🌀

Hallucinations

Confident, fluent output that is simply untrue. The more specialist the topic, the higher the risk.

📚

Fabricated references

AI routinely invents realistic-looking citations, authors and DOIs that don't exist. Always verify sources.

🕰️

Outdated knowledge

A model's training has a cut-off date. Without live web access it won't know recent events or the latest policy.

🎭

False confidence

It rarely says “I don't know”. Wrong answers sound exactly as assured as correct ones.

🔒

Privacy

Anything you paste in may be stored or used to train future models. Never enter personal or confidential data into public tools.

©️

Copyright

Training data and generated output raise unresolved copyright and ownership questions. Don't assume outputs are free to reuse.

🌱

Environmental impact

Training and running large models consumes significant energy and water. Thoughtful use matters.

⚠️ The most common serious mistake

Trusting fluent output because it sounds authoritative. AI's confidence is not evidence. Treat every factual claim, number, and reference as unverified until you've checked it yourself.

🚫 Never do this

Never paste student data, staff data, unpublished research, personal information, or anything confidential into a public AI tool. If in doubt, leave it out — and check SETU's guidance in Topic 7.

Self-check: An AI tool gives you a journal reference that looks perfect for your report — right authors, plausible title, a real-looking DOI. What should you do?

Correct — always verify. Fabricated references are one of the most common and damaging AI errors. A real-looking DOI proves nothing; check it in a trusted database. (Asking the model “are you sure?” is not a reliable check — it may confidently defend a made-up source.)

Four ideas that underpin responsible use

🧑‍⚖️

Human oversight

A person stays accountable for every decision and output. AI advises; humans decide and sign off.

🔍

Transparency

Be open about when and how AI was used, especially in teaching, assessment and research.

🎓

Academic integrity

Use that respects assessment rules and gives proper credit — never passing AI work off as unaided.

🧠

Critical thinking

AI should sharpen your judgement, not replace it. Question, verify, and think for yourself.

SETU

Responsibility stays with you

Whatever a tool produces, the professional judgement — and the accountability — remain human. That principle sits behind everything in SETU's guidance.

Topic 8

Prompting basics

Not “prompt engineering” — just a simple, repeatable habit.

⏱️ ~6 min📖 Read

You don't need special tricks or magic phrases. Getting good results is mostly a loop of four plain-English steps — with your own judgement wrapped around all of it.

1️⃣

Ask

Give clear context: who it's for, what you want, how long, what tone. Specifics beat cleverness.

2️⃣

Refine

Treat the first reply as a draft. Tell it what to change — “shorter”, “more formal”, “add an example”.

3️⃣

Review

Check it for accuracy, tone, and anything invented. Verify every fact and reference.

4️⃣

Reflect

Is this actually better than doing it yourself? Is it appropriate to use AI here at all?

A weak prompt vs a good one

🚫 Weak

“Write about assessment.”

✅ Better

“Draft a 150-word, friendly email to first-year students explaining how our assessment deadlines work this semester. Plain English, no jargon, encouraging tone.”

⚠️ The non-negotiable

Always keep human judgement in the loop. The four steps produce a draft; you decide whether it's accurate, appropriate, and fit to use.
